Meta's WhatsApp Business Agent Stopped Being Free. Here's What It Actually Costs.

The AI receptionist is now built into the platform your customers already use — and as of August 1, it has a price tag. What Israeli small businesses should check before switching it on.

On June 3, at its Conversations conference in London, Meta flipped a switch it had been quietly testing for almost two years: the Meta Business Agent, an AI that answers your customers for you inside WhatsApp, Instagram and Messenger, went live for businesses worldwide. Meta says more than a million businesses were already running one before the global rollout.

I build WhatsApp bots for Israeli businesses for a living, so you might expect me to tell you this is terrible news and quietly hope you never notice the feature exists. It's the opposite. Meta building an AI receptionist into the platform is the strongest signal yet that this category is real. But after 50-plus bot deployments into Israeli businesses, I can also tell you where the built-in agent will shine, where it will quietly embarrass you, and what to check before you let it talk to your customers.

What Meta actually shipped

The Business Agent answers customer questions, recommends products from your catalog, books appointments, qualifies incoming leads, and hands the conversation to a human when it hits a threshold you define. There is nothing to install and nothing to integrate: it learns from your Facebook page, your past chats, and your website, if you have one.

You also control how much rope it gets — from drafting replies you approve, up to handling conversations on its own until it escalates. That escalation threshold is where most of the risk lives, and we'll come back to it.

The free window already closed — and the number is smaller than you'd guess

Most of the coverage you'll find still describes the agent as free. That stopped being true on August 1, when the test window that ran through July ended and token billing began. Meta charges a single global rate of $2.00 per million tokens — tokens being the units of text the model reads and writes.

That sounds abstract until you convert it. A delivered AI message runs somewhere in the range of 20,000–25,000 tokens once you count the business context the model reads before it answers. At $2.00 per million, that's roughly four to five cents per conversation. A shop fielding 300 AI-handled conversations a month is looking at something under $15.

Here is the part worth sitting with. On our own client accounts, a marketing template message to an Israeli number costs roughly seven times what a utility message costs — and a single one of those marketing sends lands in the same few-cents range as an entire AI conversation. Meta didn't price this to be a profit center yet. They priced it to make refusing it feel irrational.

Which it mostly is. Just don't let "cheap" do your thinking for you: the cost of this feature was never going to be the invoice. It's what the agent says while nobody's watching.

Where it will genuinely help

If half of your WhatsApp day is copy-pasting the same six answers — opening hours, do you deliver to Ashdod, is this in stock, how do I get to you — the built-in agent pays for itself at four cents a conversation without you having to think about it twice. For a one-person Israeli business drowning in repetitive questions, "turn on the built-in AI" is honestly the right first step, and I say that as someone who sells the alternative.

The Israeli fine print: three things to check first

1. Hebrew. Meta's launch materials say the agent learns from your page and your website. They say nothing about Hebrew quality. In our own testing of AI models for Hebrew, in both chat and voice, the gap between the English demo and Hebrew production shows up fast: gendered address that alienates half your customers, slang it takes literally, and confident answers to questions it misread. Before you let it near customers, test it in Hebrew with real customer phrasing — the messy, typo-ridden, no-punctuation kind — not the tidy sentences from your FAQ page.

2. The spam law. Israel's anti-spam law allows statutory damages of up to ₪1,000 per unsolicited marketing message, with no need to prove actual harm. An agent that "qualifies incoming leads" is an agent that sends commercial messages on your behalf. If it follows up with someone who never gave consent, you are the one liable — not Meta. The law does not care whether a human or a model pressed send. Whatever automation you use, built-in or custom, your consent records need to be in order first.

3. The answers it invents from stale data. Last month, a home seller in the middle of a conversation with one of our real-estate listing bots stopped answering questions and asked one of his own: "Who are you?" Customers probe. They test. And an agent that learned your business from your website will answer with whatever your website says — so if your site still lists 2023 prices, the agent will quote them, confidently, in writing, to a customer who screenshots it. Feed it current information or don't feed it at all.

Escalation is the whole game

The businesses that get burned by this won't be burned because the AI answered "what are your hours" badly. They'll be burned because it answered a price objection, a complaint, or a cancellation request on its own. The single most important setting in the entire product is the threshold where a human takes over.

My advice: start with the agent drafting and you approving. Watch what it wants to say for two weeks. Let it run unattended only on the question types where its drafts were consistently right, and keep price, complaints, and anything involving money on the human side of the line.

Where a custom bot still wins

To be fair to my own trade: the built-in agent lives entirely inside Meta's walls. It can't create an invoice in your Israeli billing software, check availability in your actual calendar system, push a lead into your CRM, or run a multi-step flow like deposit-then-booking-then-reminder. As an Israeli WhatsApp bot developer I'm obviously biased, so discount accordingly — but here is the pattern I expect over the next year: Meta's agent becomes the entry point, businesses discover what conversational automation does to their response times, and then they hit the walls and want the version that talks to their own systems.

Bottom line

The AI receptionist just moved from "a thing agencies sell you" to "a line item on your Meta bill that costs less than lunch." That's good for everyone. Turn it on — with a human approving drafts at first, tested in Hebrew, with your consent records in order and your website prices current — and read the fine print before your customers write it for you.
